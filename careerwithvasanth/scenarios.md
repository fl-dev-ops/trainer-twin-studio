Interview 1: Resume Mastery

Objective

The goal of this interview is to make the candidate completely confident and authentic about everything mentioned in their resume.

The three interviews should progressively move from understanding the resume → validating impact → challenging the candidate's claims.

---

Round 1: Project & Experience Deep Dive

Focus: Understanding what the candidate actually worked on.

The interviewer should select important projects or experiences from the candidate's resume and progressively drill down into them.

The discussion should cover:

- What was the project and what problem was it solving?
- What was the candidate's specific role and responsibility?
- What exactly did the candidate build or contribute?
- What technologies and architectural decisions were involved?
- Why were certain technical decisions made?
- What challenges did the candidate face?
- What alternatives were considered?
- What was the candidate's individual contribution versus the team's contribution?
- What was the outcome of the project?

The interviewer should progressively go deeper rather than asking disconnected questions.

Example:

«"You mentioned that you built a payment system."»

→ What exactly did you build?

→ Why was this approach chosen?

→ What were the major technical challenges?

→ What happened when the system failed?

→ What part did you personally own?

→ If you had to build it again, what would you change?

Goal: By the end of this round, the candidate should be able to explain their projects with clarity and demonstrate genuine ownership rather than simply repeating keywords from their resume.

---

Round 2: Impact & Quantification

Focus: Validating the measurable impact claimed on the resume.

Many resumes contain statements such as:

- "Improved productivity by 8%"
- "Reduced page load time by 30%"
- "Improved performance by 40%"
- "Reduced costs by 20%"
- "Increased user engagement by 15%"

The interviewer should challenge these numbers and understand whether the candidate genuinely knows how the impact was measured.

Questions should explore:

- What was the baseline?
- What changed after your work?
- How exactly was the improvement measured?
- What metric or tool was used?
- Over what time period was it measured?
- How do you know the improvement was caused by your work?
- Were there other factors that could have influenced the result?
- What was the actual business or engineering impact?
- How confident are you in the number?

Example:

«"You mentioned that your solution improved productivity by 8%. How did you arrive at that number?"»

→ What was productivity before the change?

→ How did you measure it?

→ Who defined the metric?

→ How many users/teams were included?

→ What changed technically?

→ How do you know the 8% improvement came from your work?

→ What would happen if we removed your solution?

Goal: Teach candidates not to put arbitrary numbers on their resumes and ensure that every quantified claim can be confidently explained and defended.

---

Round 3: Resume Defense & Cross-Examination

Focus: Testing whether the candidate genuinely owns and understands everything they have written.

The interviewer should move across different sections of the resume and connect claims together.

Instead of simply asking about individual projects, the interviewer should challenge the candidate's ownership, consistency, depth, decision-making, and credibility.

The interviewer can:

- Pick seemingly minor claims and drill into them.
- Challenge why a particular technology or approach was chosen.
- Compare claims made in different parts of the resume.
- Ask what the candidate would do differently today.
- Challenge whether the candidate's contribution was actually responsible for the stated outcome.
- Introduce hypothetical changes and ask how the solution would behave.
- Move from high-level statements into technical details.
- Test whether the candidate can explain concepts in simple terms.

Example:

«"You mentioned that you designed the architecture for this application."»

→ What were the major architectural decisions?

→ Why did you choose this architecture?

→ What alternatives did you evaluate?

→ What part did you personally design?

→ What was the biggest limitation of your approach?

→ If the traffic increased 10x, what would break first?

→ You also mentioned improving performance. How is that connected to this architecture?

Goal: By the end of this round, the candidate should be able to confidently defend their resume under pressure and demonstrate that the resume represents their actual experience, contribution, understanding, and impact.

---

Overall Progression

Round 1 — Understanding:
"Do you genuinely understand the work you have mentioned?"

↓

Round 2 — Validation:
"Can you prove and quantify the impact you have claimed?"

↓

Round 3 — Defense:
"Can you defend everything on your resume when challenged from different angles?”















Interview 2: Fundamentals & Depth

Objective

The goal of this interview is to determine whether the candidate genuinely understands the fundamentals behind the technologies and concepts they claim to know.

The three rounds should progressively move from checking foundational knowledge → testing conceptual understanding → drilling into deeper technical reasoning and application.

---

Round 1: Fundamental Knowledge

Focus: Establish whether the candidate has strong fundamentals.

Start with concepts relevant to the candidate's role and resume. Questions should begin at a basic level and gradually cover the breadth of important fundamentals.

For a frontend candidate, this could include:

- JavaScript fundamentals
- Browser and web fundamentals
- HTML/CSS
- React fundamentals
- Networking basics
- APIs and HTTP
- Basic performance concepts

The interviewer should avoid simply asking definitions. Instead, questions should check whether the candidate can explain a concept clearly and correctly.

Example:

«"What happens when you click a button in a web application?"»

→ What happens in the browser?

→ How does JavaScript execute?

→ How does an event reach the handler?

→ What happens if the handler makes an API call?

The interviewer can use the candidate's answer to decide where to drill deeper.

Goal: Identify whether the candidate has the foundational knowledge required to reason about more advanced concepts.

---

Round 2: Conceptual Understanding

Focus: Move beyond "what" and test whether the candidate understands how and why things work.

Once a fundamental concept is identified, progressively ask questions that expose the candidate's mental model.

Questions should explore:

- Why does something work this way?
- How does it work internally?
- What happens in different scenarios?
- What are the trade-offs?
- When would you use one approach over another?
- What happens at the edge cases?

Example:

«"You mentioned that JavaScript is asynchronous."»

→ What exactly is asynchronous?

→ Is JavaScript itself asynchronous?

→ What role does the event loop play?

→ What is the call stack?

→ Where do promises execute?

→ How are microtasks different from macrotasks?

→ What happens when there are many microtasks?

The interviewer should follow the candidate's answers rather than rigidly following a predefined question list.

Goal: Distinguish between candidates who have memorized concepts and candidates who have built a correct mental model of how the system works.

---

Round 3: Depth, Reasoning & Application

Focus: Push the candidate beyond theoretical knowledge and test whether they can apply fundamentals to unfamiliar situations.

The interviewer should take a concept the candidate appears comfortable with and introduce increasingly complex scenarios.

Questions can involve:

- Debugging
- Performance problems
- Unexpected behaviour
- Architecture decisions
- Edge cases
- Trade-offs
- Hypothetical scenarios
- Combining multiple fundamental concepts

Example:

«"Your React application is becoming slow when rendering a large list. What would you investigate?"»

→ How would you identify the bottleneck?

→ What happens during rendering?

→ What causes a component to re-render?

→ How does reconciliation work?

→ When would memoization help?

→ When would it not help?

→ What if the data itself changes frequently?

→ Would virtualization solve the problem?

The interviewer should continue drilling until the candidate reaches the boundary of their understanding.

Goal: Determine the candidate's depth of knowledge and ability to reason from first principles, rather than their ability to recall prepared interview answers.

---

Overall Progression

Round 1 — Fundamentals
"Do you know the foundational concepts?"

↓

Round 2 — Understanding
"Do you actually understand how and why they work?"

↓

Round 3 — Depth & Application
"Can you use those fundamentals to reason through unfamiliar and complex problems?"

Core Principle

The interviewer should continuously adjust the depth based on the candidate's answers.

A strong candidate should naturally be taken deeper. A candidate with shallow knowledge should be exposed through follow-up questions.

The objective is not to ask the hardest possible questions. The objective is to discover how deep the candidate's understanding actually goes.











Interview 3: Real-World Problem Solving & System Design

Objective

The goal of this interview is to evaluate how the candidate approaches ambiguous, real-world engineering problems rather than testing their ability to recall predefined system-design answers.

The three rounds should progressively move from understanding the problem → designing a solution → handling scale, trade-offs, and unexpected constraints.

---

Round 1: Problem Understanding & Requirement Gathering

Focus: Evaluate how the candidate approaches an unfamiliar problem before jumping into a solution.

The interviewer should present a realistic problem with intentionally incomplete requirements.

The candidate should be expected to:

- Clarify the problem statement.
- Identify functional requirements.
- Identify non-functional requirements.
- Ask relevant questions about users, scale, traffic, data, and constraints.
- Identify assumptions.
- Define what is in scope and out of scope.
- Break the problem into smaller components.

The interviewer should avoid giving all requirements upfront. Important information can be revealed based on the candidate's questions.

Example:

«"Design a system that allows users to upload and share large files."»

A strong candidate should start asking:

→ Who are the users?

→ How large can files be?

→ How many users do we expect?

→ Are uploads resumable?

→ Can multiple users access the same file?

→ Do we need real-time progress?

→ What are the security requirements?

→ How long should files be retained?

Goal: Evaluate whether the candidate can understand the actual problem before attempting to solve it.

---

Round 2: System Design & Solution Development

Focus: Evaluate how the candidate converts requirements into a practical technical solution.

Once the requirements are established, the candidate should design the system progressively.

The discussion can cover:

- High-level architecture
- Major components
- Data flow
- APIs
- Data storage
- Caching
- Communication between services
- Frontend/backend responsibilities
- Error handling
- Security
- Performance considerations
- Technology choices

The interviewer should ask the candidate to explain why each major decision was made.

Example:

For the file-upload system:

→ How would the frontend upload the file?

→ Would the file go through the backend?

→ How would you handle large files?

→ How would resumable uploads work?

→ Where would files be stored?

→ How would another user download the file?

→ How would you prevent unauthorized access?

→ How would the frontend show upload progress?

Goal: Evaluate whether the candidate can create a coherent end-to-end system, rather than listing technologies or drawing disconnected boxes.

---

Round 3: Scale, Trade-offs & Real-World Challenges

Focus: Test how the candidate reacts when the initial design is challenged.

Once the candidate has proposed a solution, the interviewer should introduce new constraints and unexpected scenarios.

For example:

«"Your system is now being used by 10 million users."»

Then progressively introduce challenges:

- Traffic increases 10x.
- A critical service becomes unavailable.
- Database performance degrades.
- A particular API becomes a bottleneck.
- Users experience high latency in certain regions.
- A large number of users perform the same operation simultaneously.
- Data consistency becomes an issue.
- Infrastructure costs become too high.
- A security vulnerability is discovered.

The candidate should explain:

- What breaks first?
- How would they identify the bottleneck?
- How would they modify the architecture?
- What trade-offs are introduced?
- What would they prioritize?
- What would they monitor?
- How would they handle failure?
- What would they deliberately not optimize?

The interviewer can also challenge previous decisions:

«"Earlier you chose approach A. Now that traffic has increased 10x, would you still choose it?"»

Goal: Determine whether the candidate can reason under changing constraints, understand trade-offs, and evolve a system rather than simply presenting a pre-prepared architecture.

---

Overall Progression

Round 1 — Understand
"Can you identify what problem actually needs to be solved?"

↓

Round 2 — Design
"Can you create a technically sound solution for that problem?"

↓

Round 3 — Evolve & Reason
"Can you adapt your solution when scale, failures, constraints, and trade-offs are introduced?"

Core Principle

The interviewer should not evaluate the candidate only on the final architecture.

The important signal is how the candidate thinks:

Clarify → Break down → Design → Explain trade-offs → Identify weaknesses → Adapt

A strong candidate may not arrive at the "perfect" architecture immediately. What matters is whether they can reason logically, identify problems in their own design, and improve it as new information becomes available.
